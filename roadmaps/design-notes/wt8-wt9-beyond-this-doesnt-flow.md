# Beyond "This Doesn't Flow"

### Teaching paragraph coherence with Williams' running subject, and where it sits among the other tools

*A working paper for the tutor toolkit. Written to be read later by the author, and by tutors who want to understand why the flow-and-coherence tool (WT8) is built the way it is — and why it is not just another "improve your flow" exercise.*

---

## 1. The problem this addresses

Almost every tutor, at some point, writes "this doesn't flow" in a margin. It is one of the most common pieces of feedback on student writing, and one of the least useful. The student reads it, agrees that the paragraph doesn't flow, and has no idea what to *do*. "Flow" names a feeling the reader has; it does not name a thing the writer can find and fix. The same is true of its cousins: "make this connect," "this is a bit disjointed," "improve the cohesion here." All of them describe the reader's experience and leave the writer to guess at the cause.

The feedback fails not because it is wrong but because it stops at the level of *symptom*. The paragraph genuinely doesn't flow. But "flow" is an effect, and the tutor has named the effect without naming the mechanism that produced it. The student is left to reverse-engineer the cause from the symptom, which is exactly the thing they could not do in the first place — if they could feel where the flow broke and why, they would not have written it that way.

What follows is an account of a method that names the mechanism, why it works pedagogically, and the design considerations that come with turning it into a teaching tool rather than an editing one.

## 2. Williams' insight: coherence is mostly visible at the start of sentences

Joseph Williams, in *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace*, did something most writers on composition do not. He took the vague, felt quality of "flow" and located a concrete, observable feature of the text that largely produces it. This is the move that matters, and it is worth being precise about it.

Williams distinguished two things that are usually blurred together:

**Cohesion** is local. It is the sense, sentence to sentence, that each sentence connects to the one before it. It is felt as *flow*.

**Coherence** is global. It is the sense that a whole paragraph hangs together as being *about* one thing. It is felt as *focus*.

His central observation is about where coherence comes from. Readers decide what a passage is *about* mainly from what sits at the **start of each sentence** — the opening slot, which is most often filled by the sentence's grammatical subject. (Williams calls this opening slot the "topic position" of the sentence. The name is unfortunate for our purposes, because that slot is usually occupied by the grammatical *subject*, not by the "topic" in the everyday sense of what the paragraph concerns. To avoid the trap, this paper says "the start of the sentence" or "the subject position" and reserves *topic* for the everyday meaning — what a thing is about.) The grammatical subject is not "what the sentence is about"; it is the person or thing that *does the verb*. In *the council withdrew the funding*, the subject is *the council*, because the council does the withdrawing — regardless of what the sentence, or the paragraph, is "about".

Now the powerful part. If you take a paragraph, extract the subject of every sentence, and lay those subjects out as a list, the list itself tells you whether the paragraph will feel coherent. When the subjects form a **consistent, related set**, the paragraph reads as focused. When they **scatter** across unrelated things, the paragraph feels like it wanders — *even if every individual sentence is perfectly clear and correct.* The incoherence is not in any one sentence. It is in the pattern of openings, which only becomes visible when you look at them together.

This is why Williams is not, as one might dismiss it, "trivia" or one more style-guru's list of preferences. Most composition advice stays at the level of exhortation: *vary your sentences, make it cohere, improve your transitions.* Williams found the lever you can actually pull. He converted an abstract virtue into a mechanical procedure a student can repeat: list your subjects, look at the list. That conversion — from felt quality to observable operation — is the whole pedagogical value.

## 3. The companion rule: old before new

Subject consistency is half the picture. The other half is the rule that governs the *joins* between sentences, and it explains why a paragraph can legitimately *change* subject without losing the reader.

Readers absorb a sentence most easily when it **begins with information they already have** and **ends with information that is new**. The familiar opening gives them somewhere to stand; the new material at the end is what the next sentence can pick up. This produces a chain: each sentence ends by introducing something, and the next sentence opens on that same something, now familiar, before introducing its own new point.

This matters enormously for teaching, because it rescues the method from a crude misreading. The naive version of "keep a consistent subject" produces frightened, monotonous prose in which every sentence starts with the same word. That is not the goal. The goal is that when the subject *does* change, the change has been *set up* — the new subject was introduced at the end of the previous sentence, so the reader is ready for it. A signalled change of subject reads as a smooth, logical move. An unsignalled one reads as a jump.

So a good paragraph is not one where every sentence has the same subject. It is one where the subjects form either a consistent set *or* a series of clean, prepared hand-offs. Distinguishing those two good cases from the bad case — the unprepared jump — is the actual skill being taught.

## 4. Why this is a "show to learn" method, not a shortcut

The flow-and-coherence tool does one thing *for* the student: it extracts and displays the subject string. It is reasonable to ask whether this gives away the learning. It does not, and the reason is worth stating, because it generalises to other tools.

The *labour* of listing subjects is mechanical, and for most students it is not where the learning sits — copying out the first few words of each sentence is largely clerical. The learning is in the *act of seeing your own subjects laid out as a chain*. When a student sees, on their own paragraph,

> *the council → this decision → local charities → the money → residents*

something clicks that no amount of being told "keep your topic consistent" achieves. They see their paragraph wander, in their own words, at a glance. This is a demonstration performed on their own writing, which is far stickier than an abstract principle or a demonstration on someone else's text.

The pedagogical bet is that after watching this extraction performed two or three times, the student internalises it and begins to run the test in their own head — at which point they no longer need the tool. That is the definition of a successful teaching tool: it makes itself unnecessary. So the tool doing the listing is not a concession; it is the method. What the tool withholds is everything that follows — the judgement (does this string cohere?) and the repair (how would you fix it?) — because those are where the learning that *transfers* actually lives.

## 5. Where this sits among the tools: the relationship to "chain of ideas"

The toolkit already contains a paragraph tool (WT2) built on a different principle: the **chain of ideas**. It asks the student to trace claim → evidence → what it shows → why it matters → final focus, and to find the *missing link* where the reasoning leaps. It is worth being honest about how these two relate, because they are easily confused and they are not the same thing.

They operate on different axes. Chain-of-ideas works at the level of *content and logic* — is the reasoning connected? Subject-coherence works at the level of *form* — are the grammatical subjects at the starts of sentences consistent enough for a reader to track? These can come apart in both directions. A paragraph can have sound logic but a scattered subject string, so the reader cannot *feel* the connections that are genuinely there. And a paragraph can have a flawless subject string while saying almost nothing — coherent on the surface, empty underneath.

So subject-coherence is not simply a better version of chain-of-ideas. But there is a real sense in which, *as a teaching instrument*, chain-of-ideas is the cruder of the two — and it is cruder in a precise and diagnosable way. Chain-of-ideas tells the student *that* a connection is missing without giving them a mechanical place to look. "Find the missing step in your reasoning" asks for a content judgement the struggling student often cannot make — because if they could see the missing step, they would have written it. Williams' method is more powerful precisely because it is *formal and observable*: you do not need to understand your own argument to list your subjects and see them jump. The surface feature is visible even when the underlying logic is not. It gives the student a lever they can pull without already being an expert.

This is the heart of why "this doesn't flow" persists as feedback. Tutors who stop there are operating at the level of the felt symptom. Tutors who reach for "develop your ideas more" are operating at the chain-of-ideas level — better, but still asking for a judgement the student may not be able to make. The running-subject test operates one level deeper still, at the observable surface, and so hands the student something they can actually do.

## 6. The crude tool comes first — but flow analysis finds the gaps: the loop

None of this means chain-of-ideas should be retired in favour of subject-coherence. The crudeness is, in fact, pedagogically useful — as a *first rung*.

Chain-of-ideas engages at the level a struggling student can reach: *ideas*, not grammar. A first-year who cannot yet think about "the grammatical subjects at the starts of my sentences" can think about "what is my example, and what does it show?" It meets the student where they are. Subject-coherence presupposes that the ideas are roughly present and roughly ordered — it makes present ideas *trackable*; it cannot supply ideas that are not there. Run the running-subject test on a paragraph whose ideas are not yet connected, and the student will produce a tidy subject string laid over incoherent content: a coherent surface with an empty middle. That is precisely the failure Williams warns against, and it is worse than the original, because it looks finished.

So the two tools ladder:

> **WT2 — chain of ideas:** *Is your reasoning connected at all?* Content-level. Meets a novice where they are. No mechanical handle — relies on good questions.
>
> **WT8 — subject coherence:** *Can a reader follow the connections you have made?* Form-level. Gives a mechanical handle. Presupposes the ideas are roughly there.

This implies a concrete design feature for any future tool that combines them. But it is easy to state that feature too crudely — as a one-way gate: *check the reasoning first, and only if it passes, run the flow pass.* That is half right, and the half it gets wrong matters.

It is right about **repair order**. You should not polish subject flow over reasoning that has not connected, or you produce the tidy-surface-over-a-hole failure above. So when a paragraph's ideas genuinely are not built yet, flow work is premature, and the student belongs in chain-of-ideas first.

It is wrong about **diagnostic order** — and this is the subtle point. Running the subject-flow test is itself one of the most reliable ways to *discover* that the reasoning is broken. So reasoning is not a prerequisite you clear before flow analysis begins; flow analysis is often the probe that finds the reasoning gaps in the first place. The mechanism is direct. When the subject string shows a jump the previous sentence did not set up, you have found one of two things, and you usually cannot tell which until you look closer:

- the writer **had** a connecting thought and simply failed to put it on the page — a true flow problem, where the link exists in the writer's head and only the hand-off is missing; or
- there **was no** connecting thought — and the unprepared jump is the visible symptom of a hole in the reasoning itself.

The subject string cannot, on its own, say which. But it reliably flags the *location*: it shows exactly where the reader falls off, and that spot is where either a missing hand-off or a missing idea lives. In this sense subject-flow analysis is an X-ray. It does not name the disease, but it shows precisely where to cut. And a scattered string very often turns out to be scattered *because* the reasoning underneath never connected — the formal symptom and the logical fault are the same break, seen from two sides.

So the honest relationship between the two tools is not a turnstile but a **loop with a repair-order rule**:

1. **Run the subject-flow test.** It locates every place the reader falls off — cheaply, mechanically, without needing to understand the argument first.
2. **At each break, ask the discriminating question:** *is a thought missing here, or just a hand-off?* Equivalently, to the writer: do you know the connection and leave it unwritten, or have you not yet worked it out?
3. **If a hand-off is missing** (the connecting idea exists), stay in subject-coherence: fix the flow.
4. **If a thought is missing** (no connecting idea), the flow problem was a reasoning problem in disguise: hand to chain-of-ideas, build the idea, and only then can the flow question even be asked.

There is one crucial limit that keeps this honest. Subject-flow exposes reasoning gaps **reliably as locations, unreliably as verdicts.** It tells you *where* to look with high confidence; it is much weaker at telling you *whether* what you found is a missing idea or a missing link, because that judgement requires reading the content — which is chain-of-ideas' job, and ultimately the writer's. The danger, then, is a tool or a tutor that sees a scattered string and confidently announces "your argument is incoherent." Sometimes the argument is sound and only the hand-offs are missing. The string flags the spot; it does not get to deliver the diagnosis. This is the same humility built into the standing labels elsewhere in the toolkit: the *extraction* is mechanical and trustworthy, but the *interpretation* of what a break means is a judgement that stays with a human.

The repair-order rule still does real work — it stops a reasoning-broken paragraph from receiving a false polish, which would teach the student to produce convincing-looking emptiness. But it is a rule about *what to fix first*, not a gate that must be cleared before the flow tool may run. The flow tool runs early, precisely because it is the instrument that finds the gaps.

## 7. Considerations for tutors using the tool

A few practical cautions follow from all of the above.

**Do not let the test become a rule against changing subjects.** The "clear follow-on" case — a signalled, prepared change of subject — is good writing. A student who comes away believing every sentence must start with the same word has learned the wrong lesson and will write worse, not better. The three-way distinction (consistent / signalled follow-on / unprepared jump) is the lesson, not "keep the subject the same."

**Precision before polish still governs.** A change of subject that carries a real change of meaning is the student's to keep. Do not push a student to merge two subjects, or flatten a distinction, merely to make the subject string look tidy. The test serves the reader's understanding, not neatness.

**The tool meets the student; the student does the judging.** The extraction is shown; the diagnosis and repair are the student's. The questions that follow the subject string must be genuine questions from the reader's seat ("where, as a reader, do you lose the thread?"), not rewrites in disguise, and they must permit the student to conclude that a given change is fine. The moment a question is a hidden instruction, it has stopped teaching judgement and started fishing for compliance.

**Watch for the prerequisite.** If a student's sentences are individually unclear, subject-coherence is premature — send them to sentence-level clarity work first. If the ideas are not yet connected, send them to chain-of-ideas first. Subject-coherence is a finishing skill, and finishing skills come last.

## 8. Summary

"This doesn't flow" names a symptom. Williams' contribution is to name the mechanism beneath it: readers track a paragraph through the subjects at the starts of sentences, and a paragraph coheres when those subjects form a consistent set or a series of prepared hand-offs. That mechanism is *observable* — you can list the subjects and see the problem — which is what makes it teachable in a way that "improve your flow" never is.

The tool built on it does one thing for the student (shows the subject string, as a demonstration on their own writing) and leaves everything else to them. It sits alongside chain-of-ideas, which is the cruder but more accessible first rung: the two are not a one-way gate but a loop, because flow analysis is itself one of the best ways to find where the reasoning has broken. The rule that survives is one of repair order, not sequence — do not polish flow over a hole in the argument — while the flow test runs early, precisely because it is the instrument that locates the holes. Used this way, the method moves both the tutor and the student past the margin note that helps no one, and gives them instead a test they can run for themselves on any paragraph they ever write.

## 9. A precise account of the faults — and one teaching trap

Two refinements matter enough to set down carefully, because both are places where a loose version of this method does harm.

### 9.1 What actually goes wrong (it is not "abstract subjects")

It is tempting to summarise the fault as "abstract subjects are bad — use concrete actors." This is wrong, and worth correcting explicitly. If a paragraph is genuinely about an abstraction — inflation, an ideology, a legal principle — then that abstraction *is* the actor, and putting it in the subject position is correct. "Inflation eroded household savings" has an abstract subject and is perfectly clear, because inflation is the thing doing the eroding. Williams has no objection to abstract subjects as such.

The real faults are structural — about *where the doer sits and whether the reader is carried to it* — not about how abstract the word is:

- **The doer is displaced.** The real actor is in the sentence but not in the subject position: pushed into the object, into a "by..." phrase, or buried inside a noun (a nominalisation). The sentence opens on something other than the actor the paragraph is tracking.
- **The doer is delayed.** The subject is right, but the reader waits through throat-clearing or a long subordinate run-up before reaching it.
- **The subject jumps.** The running string scatters — the coherence fault proper.
- **The subject is not handed off to.** The opening lands on something the previous sentence never set up, violating old-before-new.

Naming these four precisely is what lets the tool diagnose rather than gesture. "Your subjects are too abstract" is advice the student cannot reliably act on, and is sometimes simply false. "The doer of this sentence is sitting in the object position, so the reader's eye lands in the wrong place" is a structural observation they can find and fix.

### 9.2 The protected passive

This is the qualification that separates a Williams-literate tool from a school-rule one. The passive voice is **not** a fault to be flagged on sight. Its great legitimate use is precisely *cohesion*: a passive lets a sentence open on old, known information — keeping the subject string consistent — even though doing so moves the actor out of the subject position. "The ball is kicked by the boy" has the boy as the real doer but the ball as the subject; if the paragraph is following the ball, that is the correct choice.

So "the actor is not the subject" is only a fault when the displacement is *not* buying a cleaner hand-off or a steadier topic string. A tool that strips out passives mechanically will destroy exactly the constructions that are holding a paragraph together — and will contradict what careful grammar teaching (including the ENG101 material this toolkit draws on) already tells students about why the passive exists. The passive is a tool for managing the subject string, not an error to be hunted.

### 9.3 The teaching trap: "subject" is not "topic"

Finally, a hazard specific to teaching this method, and specific to teaching it *through an AI*. The single most damaging thing a tutor or tool can do here is to define the grammatical subject as "what the sentence is about". That definition describes the **topic**, not the subject, and in this method the two must be held rigidly apart — because the entire technique is to compare the *grammatical subjects* (a consistent string of them is good) against the *topic* (the one thing the paragraph is about). If the student thinks subject *means* topic, they will "extract subjects" by writing down what each sentence concerns, and the test collapses into nonsense.

The grammatical subject must instead be taught by the **doing test**: find the verb (the doing or being word), then ask who or what is doing it. "The boy kicks the ball" — who kicks? The boy. That is pointable and non-circular; "what it's about" is neither.

## 10. The fixed text the tool carries, and why it is fixed

Section 9.3 argued that the lesson must be *pinned* rather than generated. This section sets out exactly what is pinned and where it comes from, because the precision is the point: a tool that teaches the subject/topic distinction loosely is worse than one that does not teach it at all, since a confident wrong definition is harder to dislodge than no definition.

The teaching text in the tool is drawn from the toolkit's own grammar primer, ENG101, which has the advantage of already having solved this problem for a student audience and of being consistent with everything else the student is taught. Three pieces are carried verbatim, or close to it.

**The definition, by the doing test.** ENG101 defines the subject as the person or thing that does the verb — not by what the sentence concerns. The tool uses this and only this. The operational form is a two-step the student can perform mechanically: find the verb (the doing or being word), then ask *who or what is doing it?* In "the boy kicks the ball", find the verb — *kicks* — then ask who kicks: the boy. That is the subject. The test is pointable and non-circular, which "what the sentence is about" is not.

**The explicit quarantine of the topic.** ENG101 states plainly that the subject is a technical grammatical term and, in its own words, that it does not mean the topic or the main point of the sentence. The tool reproduces this warning rather than trusting itself to remember it, because the whole method depends on it. The technique is to hold two different things side by side: the string of *grammatical subjects* (which we want to be consistent) and the *topic* (the single thing the paragraph is about). If those two collapse into one in the student's mind, the student will "extract subjects" by writing down what each sentence concerns — and will then find, falsely, that the subjects are perfectly consistent (because they are all "about" the paragraph's topic) while the actual grammatical subjects scatter unnoticed. The test does not merely become unreliable; it inverts, reporting coherence where there is none.

It is worth naming why the confusion is so easy. Williams' own term for the sentence-opening slot is the "topic position" — and the slot is normally filled by the grammatical *subject*, not by the topic-as-aboutness. The vocabulary itself invites the collision. The tool sidesteps this by never asking the student to think about "the topic position" at all; it asks only for the grammatical subject, defined by the doing test, and keeps the word *topic* for the everyday meaning.

**The protected passive.** ENG101 also supplies the passive example the tool reuses: in "the ball is kicked by the boy", the grammatical subject is "the ball", even though the boy does the kicking. This is carried for a specific reason that only emerges in the flow tool. When we list subjects, we list what sits in the subject position — so we list "the ball", not "the boy". This is not a quirk to be corrected; it is the mechanism by which a passive can *serve* the subject string. If the paragraph is following the ball, the passive is the right choice, because it keeps "the ball" in the subject position and the reader's eye where it belongs. A tool that did not carry this would default to the school-rule ("passive is weak") and would advise the writer to "fix" the very construction holding their paragraph together.

The general principle behind all three: where a concept is both essential to a method and reliably mis-taught by an unaided model, the safest design is not a better prompt asking the model to explain it well, but fixed source text the model is instructed to present rather than paraphrase. The model's job becomes delivery, not authorship, of the part of the lesson it would otherwise degrade. ENG101 is the authored source; the tool is the delivery mechanism; and the distinction between subject and topic — the hinge the whole method turns on — is thereby stated exactly the same way every time, instead of being re-improvised, and re-confused, on each run.

## 11. The skill beneath the method: a parsing tutor

Carrying fixed teaching text inside the flow tool solves one problem — it stops the *definition* from drifting. It does not solve a second: a student may read a correct definition of the grammatical subject and still be unable to *find* one in their own writing. The flow tool teaches the subject only in passing, in service of flow. For a student who cannot reliably parse a sentence, that passing instruction is not enough; they will bounce off the running-subject test no matter how well its definition is pinned, because the test asks them to do something they cannot yet do.

This exposes a prerequisite that sits below the whole strand of writing tools. Identifying the subject and verb is not unique to the running-subject method. Sentence-level clarity work needs it (is the doer the grammatical subject?). Sentence completeness needs it (a fragment is precisely a group of words missing a subject or a verb). The running-subject test needs it most acutely of all. The skill is a genuine foundation, and the toolkit's philosophy — grammar always in service of writing, never grammar for its own sake — means that foundation should still be taught the toolkit's way: briefly, on the student's own sentences, and pointed at a use.

The answer is a dedicated parsing tutor, distinct in kind from the analysis tools. Where the flow tool *uses* the subject and withholds the rest, the parsing tutor *teaches the subject as the thing itself* — find the verb, ask who or what is doing it, see the actor behind the form, recognise when a sentence is complete — and has the student practise on their own writing until they can do it without the scaffold. It is scoped tightly: only the parts the writing tools actually draw on (verb, subject, object enough to locate the subject by contrast, and the actor/subject gap that matters for the passive), not a full parsing course, which composition research has long found transfers poorly to better writing when taught in the abstract.

Two design choices give it its character. First, it teaches on the student's *own* sentences from the start, using neutral examples only to introduce a term before the student finds it in their writing — so the grammar never leaves sight of its use. Second, it is both a tool a student can choose and an **on-ramp the other tools hand off to**: when a student visibly cannot find a subject mid-way through the flow or clarity tool, that tool pauses and routes them to the parsing tutor, which teaches the skill and sends them back. This makes the prerequisite available exactly when the need for it surfaces, rather than as a gate every student must pass through first — most students already have the skill, and should not be detained.

The relationship to the rest of the strand is therefore a layering, not a queue. The parsing tutor supplies the *ability* to run the tests; chain-of-ideas supplies the *material* the tests need something to be about; the running-subject test supplies the *finishing* that makes present material trackable. A student may meet them in any order the work demands — sent down to parsing when they cannot find a subject, back to chain-of-ideas when a flow break turns out to be a missing idea, up to the flow test when the ideas are present but jump. What unites them is the same principle stated throughout this paper: name the mechanism, make it observable, and teach it on the student's own writing — so that "this doesn't flow", "this isn't a sentence", and "this isn't clear" stop being verdicts the student cannot act on, and become tests the student can run.

## 12. The reference source behind the tools: why, how, and where

Sections 10 and 11 both lean on the same body of material: a grammar primer (referred to here as ENG101) that defines the subject by the doing test, warns that the subject is not the topic, and protects the passive. It is worth setting out explicitly why a separate reference source exists at all, how it should be managed, and where it belongs — because mishandling it would quietly undo the very protections the fixed teaching text was introduced to provide.

### 12.1 Why use a reference source at all

The argument is the one developed across sections 9 and 10, generalised. There are a handful of concepts in this strand that are simultaneously *essential to the method* and *reliably degraded by an unaided model*: the definition of the grammatical subject, the subject/topic distinction, and the status of the passive. For these, the safe design is not to ask the model to explain them well, but to hand it authored text to deliver. That authored text has to live somewhere. If it lives only as snippets copied into each tool, there is no single authority for what the "correct" wording is — and the wording is exactly the thing that must not vary. A reference source is the place the canonical wording lives.

So the reference source is not documentation in the ordinary sense — not a manual a maintainer consults occasionally. It is the *source of record for the load-bearing definitions*, the thing the tools are delivering on the model's behalf. That is a stronger role than "background reading", and it is why the source deserves deliberate management rather than being left as one more file.

### 12.2 How to use it: single-source, then reference, never duplicate

The hazard is duplication. The subject/topic definition is currently needed by at least two tools — the parsing tutor and the flow tool — and will be needed by others (sentence-clarity work, a sentence-completeness tool). If each tool carries its own inline copy, the copies will drift apart the first time one is edited, and the guarantee that the definition is "stated exactly the same way every time" silently fails. The whole point of the fixed text is defeated by having several versions of it.

The disciplined alternative is to **single-source each definition** in the reference area and have the tools *point at* it rather than embed their own copy. Concretely, the reference source is broken into small extracts, one per concept — a subject/verb/object note, a sentence-completeness note, a passive note — each of which is the one canonical statement of that concept. A tool that needs the subject definition references the subject/verb/object extract; it does not paste its own.

Two cautions attach to this, both learned from the material itself:

First, **the extract process must preserve the load-bearing warnings even though they look skippable.** When a full primer is cut down to a tight extract, an editor working for concision will be tempted to drop the "the subject is not the topic" line and the "the passive is not automatically weak" line, because to anyone who already understands the method they read as redundant. They are not redundant; they are the two things the model gets wrong unaided, and so they are the precise reason the extract exists. Any extract touching subjects or the passive should mark these as do-not-cut.

Second, **the team must decide how tools consume the extracts** — by genuine reference (an assembly or include step that pulls the canonical text in at build time) or by convention (the extract is simply the one place anyone copies from when editing a tool). The first is more robust; the second is lower-tech but depends on discipline. Either is fine. What is not fine is having canonical extracts *and* independent inline copies with no rule about which wins, because that recreates the duplication the extraction was meant to remove.

### 12.3 A worked example

Suppose the reference area holds a single extract, `subject-verb-object`, whose canonical core is:

> The **subject** is the person or thing that *does the verb*. To find it: find the verb first, then ask *who or what is doing it?* "Subject" is a technical grammar term; it does **not** mean what the sentence is *about*, or its main point. *(do-not-cut: the not-the-topic warning)*

Both the parsing tutor and the flow tool need this. Under the single-source rule, neither carries its own definition. The parsing tutor references the extract to *teach* the skill — it presents the canonical text, then has the student find the subject in their own sentences. The flow tool references the same extract to *remind* the student of the skill before listing the subject string. The student therefore meets one definition, in identical words, whether they are learning to parse or testing a paragraph's flow. If the definition ever needs refining, it is changed in one place, and both tools update with it. The subject/topic confusion — the single most damaging error in the whole strand — cannot creep back through one tool's wording having drifted, because there is only one wording.

### 12.4 Where it belongs

The reference source is a maintainer asset, not student-facing content. The original primer should be kept frozen as the archived source of record; a cleaned, tool-facing derivative — course-specific framing removed, definitions and examples kept, UK conventions marked where they matter — is what the extracts are cut from. The natural home is a reference area beside the tools rather than inside the public prompt library: the library carries tools and the short fixed extracts they reference, never the whole primer, which would bloat every assembled prompt for no benefit.

Its most fitting use beyond the repository is as a model knowledge file — a reference the model consults rather than improvises — which is exactly what the source is for. A public-facing grammar reference page is a reasonable later asset, but it is downstream of cutting and cleaning the derivative, and so a "not yet" rather than a "no". The ordering principle throughout is the same: the definitions that the method cannot afford to have re-improvised are authored once, stored once, and referenced everywhere — and the reference source is simply the place that "once" lives.
